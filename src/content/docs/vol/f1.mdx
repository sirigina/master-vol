A call, a put and the stock are not three separate things. Hold any two of them at the same strike and expiry and you own a copy of the third. This lesson shows the copies with real SPY prices, and why a trading desk often prefers the copy to the original.

## Synthetic stock: a call minus a put

<Plain>

Buy the 770 call and sell the 770 put, both expiring November 27. Think about what happens at expiry:

- **SPY finishes above 770.** Your call lets you buy at 770. The put you sold expires worthless.
- **SPY finishes below 770.** The put holder makes you buy at 770. Your call expires worthless.

Either way, **you end up buying SPY at 770**. That is the same as having agreed today to buy the stock later at a fixed price. Every dollar SPY rises above 770 you gain, every dollar it falls below you lose: exactly like owning the shares.

The difference is cash. Owning SPY costs $769.66 today. The synthetic costs only the gap between the two deposits, $13.90 − $11.24 = **$2.66**, with the $770 paid at expiry.

</Plain>

<Pro>

At expiry, for any strike $$K$$,

$$
\max(S_T - K,0) - \max(K - S_T,0) = S_T - K
$$

so a long call plus a short put is a long forward at $$K$$. Before expiry the package has delta $$\Delta_C - \Delta_P = 1$$ (no dividend), zero gamma, zero vega and zero theta from volatility: all the optionality cancels. What remains is a linear exposure to $$S$$ with a deferred payment of $$K$$.

The four standard synthetics follow from the same identity, with $$\text{PV}(K) = Ke^{-rT}$$ the strike's present value (a loan of that size, repaid as $$K$$ at expiry):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Long stock} &\equiv C - P + \text{PV}(K) \\
\text{Long call} &\equiv S + P - \text{PV}(K) \\
\text{Long put} &\equiv C - S + \text{PV}(K) \\
\text{Short stock} &\equiv P - C - \text{PV}(K)
\end{aligned}
$$

A $$+\text{PV}(K)$$ term means cash set aside now to pay or receive $$K$$ at expiry; a $$-\text{PV}(K)$$ term means money borrowed now and repaid at expiry. Short versions flip every sign. Desks name positions by these equivalences: "long the synthetic" means long call / short put at one strike.

</Pro>

## Any two make the third

<SyntheticTriangle client:visible />
<SnapshotNote detail="Nov 27, 2026 expiry, 770 strike, mid prices" />

Click each corner. The recipes all come from one fact: **long call − long put = long stock − the strike**. Move any term to the other side and you get a new recipe. A long call is a long put with the stock bolted on; a long put is a long call with a short stock bolted on.

<Plain title="Why the put-plus-stock looks like a call">

Own SPY and buy a 770 put. If SPY rises, you keep the gain from the shares. If SPY falls below 770, the insurance policy pays you back down to 770. Your worst case is the payment for the insurance, less the 34 cents by which the 770 strike sits above today's price. That is the shape of a call: limited downside, open upside.

</Plain>

<Pro>

With $$\text{PV}(770) = 767.00$$ at 4.6%, the snapshot closes each recipe to the cent: synthetic call $$= S + P - \text{PV}(K) = 769.66 + 11.24 - 767.00 = 13.90$$, and synthetic put $$= C - S + \text{PV}(K) = 13.90 - 769.66 + 767.00 = 11.24$$. The Greeks follow the same identity: $$\Delta_C - \Delta_P = 1$$, and gamma and vega match at one strike and expiry. The vendor's 770 deltas, 0.541 and −0.465, sum in absolute value to 1.006, within the noise of the vendor model. That is why a desk can treat a call, a put plus stock, or a put-and-call mix as the same volatility position. They differ only in delta, cash use and exercise style.

</Pro>

## Do the copies really match?

Here is each real instrument next to its synthetic, priced at the actual November 27 mid quotes. Switch between the six instruments and strikes.

<SyntheticBuilder client:visible />
<SnapshotNote detail="Nov 27, 2026 expiry, mid prices, P/L per share at expiry" />

With interest ignored, the two lines are **parallel but $3.00 apart** for long stock at the 770 strike. The synthetic finishes lower because buying it at $2.66 and paying $770 later totals $772.66, while the shares cost $769.66. Now switch the interest toggle on. Owning shares ties up $769.66 for 31 days; the synthetic ties up only $2.66. At 4.6% the difference, about **$767 for 31 days, earns $3.00**. The lines now sit on top of each other, within a fraction of a cent at the 770 strike.

That $3.00 is not a mispricing. It is the **cost of carry**: the market charges the synthetic buyer for the financing they avoided. Try other strikes: away from 770 a leftover gap appears, about 13 cents at 760 and 53 cents at 790. Mid prices on wider markets explain some of it. The rest comes from the puts being American, which makes in-the-money puts worth more than the formula assumes, and from 4.6% being read off the 770 pair alone: an American model puts the financing rate in these quotes nearer 5% (lessons [F2](/vol/f2/) and [F6](/vol/f6/)).

<Plain>

There are two ways to own SPY on November 27. Pay $769.66 for the shares today, or hold the price with a small deposit ($2.66) and pay $770 on the day. The deposit buyer leaves about $767 in the bank for a month and collects the interest; the cash buyer gives that interest up. Count the interest, and the two routes cost the same. That is why the lines meet when the toggle is on.

</Plain>

<Pro title="Carry, precisely">

Holding stock from $$0$$ to $$T$$ costs the financing $$S\,(e^{rT}-1)$$ and, if a dividend $$D$$ goes ex in between, earns $$D$$. The synthetic avoids both. So in equilibrium

$$
C - P = S - K e^{-rT} - \text{PV}(D)
$$

which is put-call parity, the subject of [lesson F2](/vol/f2/). With $$S=769.66$$, $$K=770$$, $$r=4.6\%$$, $$T=31/365$$: $$S - Ke^{-rT} = 769.66 - 767.00 = 2.66$$, matching the quoted $$C-P$$ to the cent. SPY's next dividend goes ex in December, after the November expiry, so $$D=0$$ here.

</Pro>

## Why traders bother

<Plain>

If the copy is the same as the original, why build it? Because the copy is sometimes **cheaper, easier, or the only thing on the shelf**:

- A dealer who sold you a call can **cover it** by buying the put and the stock instead of hunting for someone selling that exact call.
- A synthetic long stock needs only $2.66 of cash per share upfront (plus margin), not $769.66.
- If the call is quoted rich compared with the put, you buy the cheaper building blocks and sell the expensive original. That is the core of option arbitrage, [lesson F3](/vol/f3/).

</Plain>

<Pro>

Desk uses of synthetics:

- **Pricing consistency.** A market maker quotes calls and puts off one volatility surface; parity ties them together, so a quote that violates it gets picked off.
- **Inventory transformation.** A position in calls can be turned into the equivalent in puts by trading stock. Delta-hedged long calls and delta-hedged long puts at the same strike are the same position (lesson [D1](/vol/d1/)).
- **Financing and borrow.** A synthetic short avoids locating stock to borrow; when borrow is expensive, puts trade rich relative to calls and the synthetic short price reflects the fee.
- **Margin and balance sheet.** Synthetic forwards use less cash than stock, which matters for funds and dealers paying for balance sheet.

The caveat: the equivalence is exact only at expiry and only for European-style exercise. American options, dividends and pin risk create small differences ([F5](/vol/f5/), [F6](/vol/f6/)).

</Pro>

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing strikes or expiries.** A synthetic needs the call and put at the same strike and the same expiry; a 760 call against a 780 put is a different position (a risk reversal).
- **Forgetting the strike payment.** The synthetic stock costs $2.66 now *plus* $770 at expiry. Comparing $2.66 with $769.66 directly makes the synthetic look absurdly cheap.
- **Calling the carry gap a mispricing.** A $3.00 difference over 31 days at a 4.6% rate is just interest.
- **Assuming a synthetic has no option risk.** It has none in theory, but American exercise and a close right at the strike can leave you with an unexpected stock position.

<Takeaway>

- Long call + short put at the same strike and expiry behaves like long stock bought at the strike.
- Any two of stock, call and put recreate the third: C − P ≈ S − K, rearranged.
- On November 27, the 770 synthetic cost $2.66 and matched SPY at $769.66 once $3.00 of interest on $767 was counted.
- Synthetics let desks cover options with other options, save cash, and spot mispriced quotes.
- The match is exact for European options without dividends; real-world frictions come later in this module.

</Takeaway>
