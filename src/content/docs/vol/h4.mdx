A theoretical edge only pays if you are still in business when it arrives. Professional option desks survive on a short list of habits: limits on each Greek, attention to *where* the risk sits, size set by what a bad day would cost, and a firm rule against tail exposure they could not survive. This lesson builds each one with SPY numbers.

## Size from the stress loss

<Plain>

The most common way traders blow up is not a bad idea; it is a **good idea at the wrong size**.

Selling a strangle on SPY collects a deposit (the premium) from other traders who want protection. Most months, nothing much happens and you keep it. But you are the insurance company, and the question that matters is: **how many policies can I write before one storm sinks me?**

Work it backwards:

1. Decide the most you are willing to lose in a realistic bad scenario, say **10%** of a $250,000 account: $25,000.
2. Price the position in that scenario. If SPY drops 10% and VIX goes to 35, one short 750/790 strangle loses about **$5,330**.
3. Divide: $25,000 ÷ $5,330 = **4 strangles**. Not 20, not "whatever margin allows".

Four strangles collect only $4,152. That feels small. It is the honest price of the risk.

</Plain>

<Pro>

Stress-based sizing replaces "how much margin do I have?" with

$$
N_{\max} = \left\lfloor \frac{L_{\text{acceptable}}}{\;-\,\Pi_{\text{stress}}\;} \right\rfloor
$$

where $$\Pi_{\text{stress}}$$ is the full-revaluation P/L of one unit in the chosen scenario. Here the scenario is an instant −10% in SPY with every strike's IV multiplied by $$35/17.52 \approx 2.0$$ (the smile keeps its shape). Results per unit, against the entry marks:

| | Credit | Stress loss | Loss ÷ credit | Max units at $25k |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Short 750/790 strangle | $1,038 | $5,330 | 5.1× | 4 |
| 720/750/790/820 iron condor | $729 | $866 | 1.2× | 28 |

The condor's wings cap its expiry loss at $2,271 per unit, so it can also be sized on its absolute worst case (11 units at $25k); the strangle has no such cap. Margin rules often permit far more: portfolio margin on a strangle is itself a scenario scan, but on the exchange's assumptions, not yours.

</Pro>

## Limits on each Greek

<Plain>

A desk gives each trader limits, like speed limits on a road:

- **Delta limit:** how much the book can act like owning (or being short) the market. Example: no more than $500,000 of SPY exposure either way.
- **Gamma limit:** how fast that exposure is allowed to change, so a big move cannot turn a hedged book into a large bet in a single afternoon.
- **Vega limit:** how much the book can make or lose per point of implied volatility.
- **Stress limit:** the worst grid scenario must stay under a fixed dollar number.

The first three are about sensitivity. The last is about survival. Most experienced risk managers trust the last one most.

</Plain>

<Pro>

Typical limit structure on an options desk:

- **Dollar delta** $$|\Delta_P S| \le D$$, hedged intraday when breached.
- **Gamma per 1% move** $$|\Gamma_P\,S/100| \le G$$ shares, the delta drift you must re-hedge after a 1% move. For the condor that is about 10 shares per unit; at 28 units, about 285 shares of SPY to trade for every 1% move.
- **Vega** limits, both **net** and **by expiry bucket** (next section).
- **Scenario loss** on the price × vol grid from [lesson H3](/vol/h3/), often with a separate crash scenario (−15% to −25%).
- **Concentration** limits: share of open interest, single strike, single expiry, and liquidity (days to unwind at normal volume).

Limits are set relative to capital and to each other: a book at its vega limit with a large short-gamma position is closer to its stress limit than either number shows alone.

</Pro>

## Concentration by expiry

<Plain>

Net vega can lie. Here is a sample book: short straddles and strangles in November, long straddles in December and March. Add up its vega and it is nearly **flat**: only −$406 per volatility point. Looks safe.

Now split it by expiry. It is **short $3,281** of vega in the November expiries and **long $2,876** in December and March. That is not "no volatility risk". It is a bet that short-dated volatility falls *relative to* long-dated volatility. If the front month spikes (as it does in every sell-off), the short side reprices much more than the long side.

Gamma tells the same story: the book's net gamma (−68 shares per dollar) sits mostly in the November 6 expiry (−42 of the −68), ten days out.

</Plain>

<Pro>

Short-dated implied vol moves more than long-dated: the term structure flattens or inverts in stress (ATM IV here runs 14.7% at 10 days, 15.9% at 143 days; in March 2026 VIX moved more than a year-out vol would). A common correction is **weighted vega**: scale each bucket's vega by $$\sqrt{30/\text{DTE}}$$ so that one "point" means a comparable shock across expiries. On that measure the sample book is **−$2,529**, six times its raw net vega and clearly short.

Desks therefore limit vega per expiry bucket and per tenor-weighted total, and they stress the term structure separately (front +8, back +3, for example). Gamma concentration in the nearest expiry is also a pin risk and liquidity risk going into expiration day (lesson [D3](/vol/d3/)).

</Pro>

## The left tail: a stress replay

<Plain>

Short volatility makes small, steady gains most of the time and occasionally gives back many months of them at once. The March 2026 drawdown is a real example: SPY fell from 695.49 in late January to **631.97** on March 30, about 9%, and VIX climbed from about 16 to **31.05**.

The figure below sells a four-week strangle 5% out of the money on a chosen date and marks it to model every day.

- **Sold February 27** (SPY 685.99, VIX 19.86): collected about $519. It expired on March 27, with SPY at 634.09, deep through the 650 put: a loss of **$1,072**, twice the credit.
- **Sold March 6**, after VIX had already jumped to 29.49: the higher premium ($1,432) gave room, and it ended up keeping almost all of it.
- **Sold March 20**: the trade then lost on the *upside* as SPY rallied nearly 10% in four weeks. The tail is not only on the downside.

Same rule, four dates, very different outcomes. The rule was not wrong; the size decides whether a bad month is a lesson or the end.

</Plain>

<Pro>

The replay is a **historical stress**, not a backtest. Each day it prices the two strikes with Black-Scholes, taking each strike's IV as the snapshot's November smile (as a ratio to VIX, by moneyness) scaled by that day's VIX close. That keeps the skew and lets the level track the market, but VIX is a 30-day SPX index, not a strike quote; treat the dollar figures as illustrative.

What it shows reliably is the structure of the left tail: marks deteriorate before expiry through vega and gamma together, the worst mark tends to coincide with the VIX peak, and short-premium P/L is a **concave** function of the path. A position sized to survive the stress scenario at entry can still be forced out at the worst mark if the trader's limits are breached first, which is why desks keep a buffer between the stress limit and the capital they actually have.

</Pro>

## Common mistakes

- **Sizing on margin or on credit.** Neither tells you what a bad day costs. Size from a stress loss you have chosen and priced.
- **Trusting net vega across expiries.** Bucket it, and weight short-dated vega more heavily.
- **Selling naked tails "because they never get there".** The 650 put is 15.5% out of the money; SPY fell 9% from peak to trough within the last year. A tail you cannot survive is not a risk you can manage.
- **Treating the stress scenario as the worst case.** It is a chosen story. Markets can do worse, and they do not wait for you to rehedge.

<Takeaway>

- Size positions from the loss in a priced stress scenario, not from margin or premium.
- Set limits on delta, gamma, vega and scenario loss; the scenario limit is the one about survival.
- Bucket vega and gamma by expiry. A flat net vega can hide a large front-month short.
- Short-volatility books have a concave left tail: small steady gains, rare large losses that arrive with rising VIX.
- Defined-risk structures (wings) trade some credit for a stress loss that grows far more slowly than the scenario.

</Takeaway>
