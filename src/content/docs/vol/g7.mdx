The previous lessons built the spreads one at a time. In practice you start from a view and have to pick one. Two traders who agree that SPY options are expensive can sensibly choose a short straddle, an iron condor, a butterfly or a ratio spread. This lesson is about how that choice gets made, and how to compare candidates fairly.

## Question 1: is implied cheap or rich?

<Plain>

Everything starts with comparing two weather forecasts: the one priced into options (implied volatility) and your own. On October 27 the November options implied about **14.0%**. SPY had moved at only about 10% over the previous 20 days and about 12.7% over the past year.

- If you expect calmer weather than the options price, you want to **sell** volatility: positions that profit when SPY moves less than expected.
- If you expect stormier weather, you want to **buy** volatility.
- If your forecast is close to implied, there is no volatility trade. A spread chosen without an edge is just paying the bid/ask.

</Plain>

<Pro>

The sign of $$\sigma_{\text{forecast}} - \sigma_{\text{implied}}$$ picks the side: positive vega/gamma spreads (long straddles and strangles, backspreads, long iron condors) when it is positive, negative (short straddles and strangles, ratio spreads, long butterflies, short iron condors) when negative. Its size, relative to forecast uncertainty and transaction costs, decides whether to trade at all. Lessons [C6](/vol/c6/) and [G2](/vol/g2/) cover forecasting and the classification of spreads by the sign of gamma and vega.

A subtlety: a long volatility position is right if realized volatility over its life exceeds implied (gamma), or if implied rises before you exit (vega). These are different bets. A long straddle held to expiry is mostly a realized-vol bet; a long 6-month option held a week is mostly an implied-vol bet.

</Pro>

## Questions 2 to 6: moves, direction, skew, term and risk

Once the side is known, five more questions narrow the family.

<Plain>

- **How will the moves come?** Steady drift and a sudden jump can produce the same overall volatility. A sudden jump is great for positions with far-out options you own (backspreads, strangles) and terrible for positions with far-out options you sold.
- **Do you have a directional lean?** Ratio spreads and backspreads tilt a volatility trade toward one side: a call backspread does best on a big rally, a put ratio spread is happiest with a modest drop.
- **Is one part of the price list mispriced?** If downside puts look overpriced relative to the rest (rich skew), sell them; if they look cheap, own them.
- **Which month?** If November looks expensive relative to December, a calendar lets you sell November and own December.
- **How much can you stand to lose?** Some positions have a known worst case (butterflies, iron condors, long options). Others don't (short straddles, ratio spreads). Writing that limit down first eliminates half the choices.

</Plain>

<Pro>

| Question | If… | Favours |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distribution of moves | jump risk, fat tails | long wings: backspreads, long strangles; avoid short wings |
| | steady, mean-reverting | short ATM gamma: short straddles, butterflies |
| Direction | upside bias | call backspread (long vol), call ratio spread (short vol) |
| | downside bias | put backspread (long vol), put ratio spread (short vol) |
| Skew | downside puts rich vs ATM | sell OTM puts: short strangle, put ratio, iron condor |
| | downside puts cheap | own OTM puts: put backspread, long strangle |
| Term structure | front month rich vs back | long calendar |
| | back month rich vs front | short calendar (the snapshot's 15.7% Nov–Dec forward vol leans this way) |
| Risk tolerance | must be capped | butterflies, condors, long options, backspreads, calendars |
| | can carry open-ended | short straddles/strangles, ratio spreads |

Some answers conflict. A short-vol trader worried about jumps should prefer a butterfly or iron condor to a short straddle even though both are short volatility; a long-vol trader who also thinks puts are rich faces a choice between owning puts that are expensive or buying a call backspread that ignores the downside. Ranking is about trade-offs, not a single right answer.

</Pro>

## Comparing candidates at equal edge

Two spreads with the same view can carry very different risks for the same expected payoff. The fair way to compare them is to scale each to the **same theoretical edge** and then look at what you have to carry.

<Plain>

Suppose your forecast says SPY options are overpriced and each candidate spread would, on average, earn you something. A short straddle earns a lot per contract but can lose a lot; a butterfly earns less per contract but its worst case is small. Comparing one contract of each is apples to oranges. Instead, ask: how many of each would I need to trade to expect the same $200, and what is the worst outcome of *that* position?

</Plain>

<Pro>

For a spread with theoretical edge $$e$$ per unit at the forecast vol, size it at $$n = E^{*}/e$$ for a target edge $$E^{*}$$, then compare risk measures of the scaled position: here, the worst P/L on November 27 for SPY within ±2 standard deviations (≈±$63), plus Greeks. The ratio $$E^{*}/|\text{worst}|$$ is a crude edge-per-unit-risk.

At an 11% forecast (3 points under implied) and $200 target edge:

| Spread | Contracts | Worst ±2 SD | Edge ÷ risk | Vega |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Long butterfly 750/770/790 | 1.8 | −$942 | 0.212 | −$53 |
| Short iron condor 740/750/790/800 | 1.8 | −$1,136 | 0.176 | −$57 |
| Short straddle 770 | 0.4 | −$1,419 | 0.141 | −$66 |
| Short strangle 750/790 | 0.5 | −$1,561 | 0.128 | −$70 |
| Put ratio 770/2×750 | 1.3 | −$2,967 | 0.067 | −$74 |
| Call ratio 770/2×790 | 1.3 | −$3,617 | 0.055 | −$78 |

At 17% (3 points over), the long strangle leads at **0.437** because its worst case is the premium, followed by the long straddle (0.213) and long iron condor (0.210); the backspreads trail because their worst case sits at the long strike. Two caveats: edge here assumes your forecast applies uniformly to every strike's IV (the skew is kept, not traded), and ±2 SD hides the tail where open-ended spreads do their damage. Lesson [H1](/vol/h1/) develops this comparison with proper risk measures.

</Pro>

The pattern holds across forecasts: **positions that cap the tail tend to deliver more edge per unit of worst-case risk**, but they need more contracts (more commissions and bid/ask) and earn less per day. Open-ended spreads earn their edge with fewer contracts and larger tails. Neither is better in general; the table makes the trade-off explicit.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing the spread before the view.** "I like iron condors" is a habit, not a view. Start from the implied-vs-forecast gap.
- **Comparing one contract of each.** Edge and risk scale with size; compare at equal edge or equal risk.
- **Ignoring skew in strike selection.** Selling the 750 put and selling the 790 call are different trades at different IVs (16.5% vs 12.2% quoted).
- **Using calendars for a level view.** A calendar is mostly a term-structure trade; a forecast that applies equally to both months moves its value far less than a straddle's ($78 vs $537 per contract for a 3-point lower forecast).

<Takeaway>

- The side (long or short volatility) comes from comparing your forecast with implied; no gap, no trade.
- Move size, direction, skew, term structure and risk tolerance then narrow the family.
- Short wings suit steady markets; long wings suit jump risk. Ratios and backspreads add a directional tilt.
- Compare candidates at equal theoretical edge, then look at worst case, Greeks and costs.
- Capped-risk spreads usually give more edge per unit of worst-case risk but need more contracts.

</Takeaway>
