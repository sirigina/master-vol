[Lesson C1](/vol/c1/) built a bell curve out of coin flips, and [lesson C2](/vol/c2/) used one to turn 14% volatility into a dollar range. But a bell curve of *prices* has a flaw: stretch it far enough and it puts real probability on SPY trading below zero. This lesson fixes that, and the fix explains several quirks of option prices.

## Prices move in percentages

<Plain>

A $10 move means something very different for a $20 stock than for a $770 one. Investors think in **percentages**: SPY "fell 2%", not "fell $15". And a stock that keeps falling 10% a year gets closer and closer to zero without ever crossing it, because each fall is 10% of a smaller number.

So instead of saying "dollar moves are bell-shaped", the standard model says "**percentage moves are bell-shaped**". The price itself then follows a curve called **lognormal**: it can never go below zero, it is bunched up on the downside, and it has a long tail stretching to the upside.

</Plain>

<Pro>

The Black-Scholes model assumes geometric Brownian motion: $$dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW$$. Its solution makes the **log return** normal:

$$
\ln\frac{S_T}{S_0} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\left(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T,\ \sigma^2 T\right)
$$

so $$S_T = S_0\,e^{X}$$ with $$X$$ normal: $$S_T$$ is **lognormal** and strictly positive. Two reasons to model log returns rather than price changes: they are scale-free (a 1% move means the same at any price level), and they **add over time**: $$\ln(S_2/S_0) = \ln(S_2/S_1) + \ln(S_1/S_0)$$. Adding independent normal pieces gives another normal, which is what makes the √time scaling of [lesson C1](/vol/c1/) work exactly in log space.

</Pro>

## Same volatility, two shapes

At the defaults, **14% volatility and 31 days** (the November 27 expiry), the two curves nearly overlap. One standard deviation of log return is only 4.1%, and over such a small range a percentage scale and a dollar scale barely differ. The lognormal median is $769.02, just 64 cents under today's price.

Now push the horizon to **5 years** and the volatility to **40%**. The difference becomes impossible to miss:

- The normal curve spills far into negative prices. It assigns a **13.2%** chance to SPY being below zero, which cannot happen.
- The lognormal curve piles up between roughly $100 and $800 and stretches a long tail past $2,000.
- Its **median is $515.92** and its peak (mode) is near $232, while its mean is still $769.66.

<Plain>

Why would the middle outcome be lower than the average? Because of the long upside tail. A few paths go up enormously (a stock can triple or quadruple) while no path can go below zero. Those big winners pull the *average* up, even though the *typical* path ends lower. In the 5-year, 40% case, about two paths in three (**67.3%**) finish below today's price, yet the average finish equals today's price.

</Plain>

<Pro>

For $$X \sim \mathcal{N}(m, s^2)$$ with $$s = \sigma\sqrt{T}$$ and $$S_T = S_0e^X$$:

$$
\text{mean} = S_0 e^{m + s^2/2}, \qquad \text{median} = S_0 e^{m}, \qquad \text{mode} = S_0 e^{m - s^2}
$$

Holding the mean at $$S_0$$ (zero drift in price terms, $$\mu = 0$$) gives $$m = -s^2/2$$, so the median is $$S_0e^{-s^2/2}$$ and $$P(S_T < S_0) = N(s/2)$$. At $$s = 0.0408$$ (14%, 31 days) that is 50.8%; at $$s = 0.894$$ (40%, 5 years), 67.3%. The gap between mean and median is the **volatility drag** $$\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2$$ per year: the reason a portfolio's compound growth rate trails its average return. Under the risk-neutral measure the drift becomes $$r - q$$ instead of zero, which shifts the whole curve but leaves the shape intact.

</Pro>

Flip the axis toggle to **Log return**. Now the solid lognormal curve is a perfect, symmetric bell: the model is symmetric in log space. The dashed normal-in-price curve becomes the lopsided one: a long tail stretching far to the left, because as the price nears zero each dollar lost is a bigger and bigger percentage, and a steep drop on the right. Its 13.2% of probability below zero has nowhere to go at all; log space has no room for negative prices.

## Up and down are not mirror images

The asymmetry is easiest to feel with a single round trip.

<SnapshotNote detail="SPY close as the starting price" />

<Plain>

If SPY rises 20% and then falls 20%, it does not end where it started. It ends **4% lower**, at about $739. The fall is 20% of a bigger number, so it takes away more dollars than the rise added. To recover from a 20% drop you need a **25%** gain; from a 50% drop, a 100% gain.

Log returns sidestep this bookkeeping problem. A rise of 20% is a log return of +18.23%; a fall of 20% is −22.31%. They add to exactly −4.08%, which is the log of the net result. That is why volatility, which is computed from log returns, treats a +X% and −X% move consistently.

</Plain>

<Pro>

For a simple return $$x$$ followed by $$-x$$: $$(1+x)(1-x) - 1 = -x^2$$. The loss is second order in $$x$$, which is why it is invisible for daily moves (a ±1% round trip costs 0.01%) and dominant for large ones. In log terms the round trip is $$\ln(1-x^2)$$. Equivalently, for symmetric log moves $$\pm u$$, the price moves are $$e^{u}-1 > 1-e^{-u}$$: equal-probability up and down moves in log space are **larger in dollars on the upside**. That is the geometric source of the lognormal's right skew.

</Pro>

## What the shape does to option prices

<Plain>

Under a lognormal model the upside has more room than the downside: SPY can rise $400 but can only fall $769.66. For two options the same dollar distance from today's price, that tilts things slightly toward the call. Over a month the effect is tiny; over years it grows.

The real market disagrees with the model in the opposite direction. Traders fear crashes more than the lognormal curve does, so they pay **more** for downside puts, not less. That disagreement is the volatility **skew**, the subject of [lesson C8](/vol/c8/).

</Plain>

<Pro>

Black-Scholes prices are expectations under the lognormal law, so the shape is built into every quote. Two consequences desks rely on:

- **Strike symmetry is in log-moneyness, not dollars.** At equal implied vol, strikes $$K_1 = Fe^{-k}$$ and $$K_2 = Fe^{k}$$ around the forward $$F$$ are symmetric in $$d_1, d_2$$ terms; strikes equidistant in dollars are not. Skew metrics are therefore quoted by delta or by $$\ln(K/F)$$.
- **The model's tail is thin on the downside.** A lognormal with 14% vol puts the 31-day risk-neutral probability of SPY below 700 under 1%. The market's put prices imply several times that ([lesson C8](/vol/c8/)). Implied volatility is the fudge factor that reconciles the two: one model, a different $$\sigma$$ for each strike.

</Pro>

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming the median equals today's price.** Even with zero expected return, the lognormal median sits below the mean, and the gap grows with $$\sigma^2T$$.
- **Treating +X% and −X% as cancelling.** A round trip loses $$X^2$$. At ±50% that is a 25% loss.
- **Using the normal model for long horizons or high volatility.** It is fine for a month at 14% and badly wrong for years at 40%.
- **Thinking the lognormal model explains the skew.** It produces a mild upside tilt; the market's skew runs the other way.

<Takeaway>

- Option models assume log returns are normal, so prices are lognormal: never negative, skewed to the right.
- With the mean fixed, the median is lower than the mean and more than half of paths end below the start.
- In log space the distribution is symmetric; in price space up moves are larger in dollars than equally likely down moves.
- Over short horizons at normal volatility the two models nearly match; over long horizons or high volatility they diverge sharply.
- A +X% move followed by −X% leaves you down X², which is why volatility is measured with log returns.

</Takeaway>
