Option pricing starts from one modest assumption: nobody knows which way the next move goes. This lesson shows what that assumption implies. Pile up enough unpredictable moves and you get a bell curve, a precise rule for how often big moves happen, and a range that grows with the square root of time.

## A walk made of coin flips

<Plain>

Stand on a line and flip a coin. Heads, step right; tails, step left. Do it 100 times. Where do you end up?

Almost never 100 steps away; that would need 100 heads in a row. Usually the heads and tails mostly cancel and you finish somewhere near the start, give or take about **10 steps**. A few walkers drift out to 20 or 25. Hardly anyone gets to 40.

Now picture thousands of walkers doing this at once and mark where each one stops. The marks pile up in a hump in the middle and thin out to both sides. That hump is the **bell curve**, and it shows up whenever many small, independent pushes add together. Daily price changes are a lot like that: news, orders and opinions pushing a little up or a little down.

</Plain>

<Pro>

Let $$X_i = \pm 1$$ with equal probability, independent. The position after $$n$$ steps is $$W_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i$$, with

$$
\mathbb{E}[W_n] = 0, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(W_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n \operatorname{Var}(X_i) = n, \qquad \operatorname{SD}(W_n) = \sqrt{n}.
$$

Variances of independent steps add; standard deviations do not. By the central limit theorem, $$W_n/\sqrt{n}$$ converges in distribution to a standard normal $$\mathcal{N}(0,1)$$ as $$n$$ grows, whatever the distribution of the individual steps (as long as they are independent with finite variance). In the limit of many tiny steps the walk becomes **Brownian motion**, the building block of the Black-Scholes model.

</Pro>

<RandomWalkSim client:visible />

Press **Flip new coins** and watch the histogram on the right fill in. With 100 steps the spread of end points settles near **10**, which is √100. Drag the steps to 400 and the spread doubles to about 20, not quadruples. The orange curves mark ±√steps at every point along the way: the cloud of walks widens like a square root, fast at first and then more slowly.

One detail for the curious: with whole-number steps the share landing inside ±√steps runs a little above 68% (about 73% for 100 steps), because the end points sit on a grid. More steps shrink that gap.

## Mean and standard deviation

<Plain>

Two numbers describe a bell curve completely.

- The **mean** is the center, where the hump peaks. For a fair coin it is zero: no direction is favored.
- The **standard deviation** (SD) is the typical distance from the center. It sets how wide the hump is.

For prices, the standard deviation of returns is what traders call **volatility**. It is the weather forecast for price swings: it tells you how big the swings tend to be, never which way they go.

</Plain>

<Pro>

For a sample $$x_1,\dots,x_n$$:

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i, \qquad s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i (x_i-\bar{x})^2}
$$

The normal density with mean $$\mu$$ and standard deviation $$\sigma$$ is

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
$$

and the probability of landing within $$k$$ standard deviations is $$N(k) - N(-k) = 2N(k) - 1$$, where $$N$$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution. In option pricing the variable that is assumed normal is the **log return** $$\ln(S_t/S_0)$$, not the price itself; lesson [C3](/vol/c3/) explains why.

</Pro>

## The 68-95-99.7 rule

The normal curve comes with fixed proportions. About **68%** of outcomes land within one standard deviation of the mean, **95%** within two, and **99.7%** within three. Switch between the bands, or choose a custom width.

<Plain>

Turn the percentages into frequencies and the rule becomes practical. If SPY's daily moves were perfectly normal at 14% volatility, one SD on a day would be about **±0.88%**, roughly $6.79.

- A move bigger than one SD would happen about **1 day in 3**. Routine.
- A move bigger than two SD (about $13.58) would happen about **1 day in 22**, roughly once a month.
- A move bigger than three SD would happen about **1 day in 370**, which is less than once a year.

That last line is where real markets misbehave. Keep reading.

</Plain>

<Pro>

The tail probabilities are $$2(1-N(k))$$: 31.7% for $$k=1$$, 4.55% for $$k=2$$, 0.27% for $$k=3$$. With 252 sessions a year, a normal model expects about 11.5 two-sigma days and 0.68 three-sigma days per year. These are the benchmarks against which "fat tails" are measured. The daily SD at annual volatility $$\sigma$$ is $$\sigma/\sqrt{252} \approx \sigma/15.9$$, the "rule of 16" covered in [lesson C2](/vol/c2/).

</Pro>

## Spreading with the square root of time

Because variances add, the range of a random walk grows with the square root of the number of steps. The same holds for prices if daily moves are independent.

<Plain>

If a typical SPY day is ±$6.79, a typical week is not 5 × $6.79 = $34. Up days and down days partly cancel, just like the coin flips. The week's typical range is √5 ≈ 2.24 times the day's, about **±$15**. A month of about 21 trading days is √21 ≈ 4.6 times, about **±$31**. A full year of 252 days is √252 ≈ 15.9 times, about **±$108**.

To make a range twice as wide you need four times as long. That single fact explains why short-dated options are so cheap relative to long-dated ones, and why time decay speeds up near expiration (lesson [D3](/vol/d3/)).

</Plain>

<Pro>

For independent, identically distributed daily log returns with SD $$\sigma_d$$, the $$n$$-day return has

$$
\operatorname{SD}_n = \sigma_d\sqrt{n}, \qquad \sigma_{\text{annual}} = \sigma_d\sqrt{252}.
$$

So at 14% annualized, $$\sigma_d = 0.14/\sqrt{252} = 0.882\%$$, the 5-day SD is $$0.14\sqrt{5/252} = 1.97\%$$ and the 21-day SD is $$0.14\sqrt{21/252} = 4.04\%$$. The scaling fails when returns are autocorrelated (trending or mean-reverting at short horizons) or when volatility itself changes over the period. Both happen in practice, which is why traders treat √t as a baseline rather than a law.

</Pro>

## Reality check: SPY is almost normal

The bell curve is a model. Here is how SPY's past year of daily moves stacks up against it.

<SpyVsNormal client:visible />
<SnapshotNote detail="256 daily log returns, Oct 20, 2025 to Oct 27, 2026" />

SPY had **192 of 256 days (75%)** within one standard deviation, more than the 68% the bell predicts. It also had **16 days** beyond two standard deviations against a predicted 11.6, and **3** beyond three against a predicted 0.7. Real returns are calmer most of the time and wilder some of the time. That pattern, fat tails, is why the bell curve is a starting point rather than the last word. [Lesson C2](/vol/c2/) shows the full histogram and what it means for option prices.

<Plain>

Think of the bell curve as a weather forecast that gets the ordinary day right but is too relaxed about storms. SPY's biggest move of the year, on March 31, 2026, was about **3.5 times** its typical daily move. A perfect bell curve expects a day that size about once in nine years; SPY delivered it within one.

</Plain>

<Pro>

Measured on the same 256 log returns, the daily SD is 0.798% (12.7% annualized), the skewness is −0.07 and the kurtosis is 3.78, an excess of **0.78** over the normal's 3. Taken alone, 16 two-sigma days against 11.6 expected is not conclusive: a Poisson count with that mean reaches 16 or more about 13% of the time. Three three-sigma days against 0.69 expected is stronger evidence, with a probability of about 3%. A common source of excess kurtosis is volatility that changes over the sample: a mix of calm and stormy normal regimes is fat-tailed overall, even if each day is drawn from a bell curve.

</Pro>

## Common mistakes

- **Scaling a daily move linearly.** Five days of ±0.88% is about ±2%, not ±4.4%. Ranges grow with √time.
- **Treating a two-SD move as rare.** At about 1 day in 22, it is a monthly event.
- **Reading the mean as a forecast of direction.** The bell curve centered at zero says nothing about which way the next move goes; it only describes sizes.
- **Trusting the 99.7% literally.** Real markets produce three-SD days several times more often than a normal curve allows.

<Takeaway>

- Many small, independent up-or-down moves add up to a bell-shaped (normal) distribution.
- The mean sets the center; the standard deviation sets the width. For returns, the annualized SD is volatility.
- About 68% of outcomes fall within ±1 SD, 95% within ±2 SD, 99.7% within ±3 SD.
- Variances add, so the range grows with √time: four times the horizon, twice the range.
- SPY is close to normal but has more quiet days and more extreme days than the bell predicts.

</Takeaway>
