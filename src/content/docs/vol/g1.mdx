A single option carries every risk at once: direction, volatility and time arrive in one package. A spread lets you keep the risk you want and hand back the rest. This lesson shows what that trade-off looks like in dollars and in Greeks.

## One view, three structures

Suppose you think SPY, at 769.66, will drift up toward 780–790 over the next month. Here are three ways to act on that with November 27 calls:

- **Long 770 call** for $13.90.
- **770/790 bull call spread**: buy the 770 call, sell the 790 call at $4.44, for a net $9.46.
- **770/780/790 call butterfly**: buy one 770, sell two 780s at $8.34, buy one 790, for a net $1.66.

<G1SpreadCompare client:visible />
<SnapshotNote detail="Nov 27, 2026 calls at mid, 1 contract = 100 shares" />

Read the table from left to right. The call costs $1,390 and has no ceiling. The vertical costs about two-thirds as much, $946, and caps the gain at $1,054. The butterfly costs $166 and can make $834, but only if SPY lands near 780; at 770 or 790 it loses everything it cost.

<Plain>

Think of each option as a **deposit that holds a price**. Buying the 770 call is putting down a deposit to buy SPY at 770. In the vertical you also *sell* someone else a deposit at 790. Their payment cuts your cost, but above 790 you have promised away the extra gains.

The butterfly goes further: you pay for two deposits (770 and 790) and sell two in the middle (780). You are no longer betting that SPY *goes up*; you are betting it ends up *near 780*. It is cheap because it is a precise bet.

Each step trades away some upside for a lower cost and a smaller worst case.

</Plain>

<Pro>

At expiry the three payoffs per share are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Call: } & (S_T-770)^+ - 13.90 \\
\text{Vertical: } & (S_T-770)^+ - (S_T-790)^+ - 9.46 \\
\text{Butterfly: } & (S_T-770)^+ - 2(S_T-780)^+ + (S_T-790)^+ - 1.66
\end{aligned}
$$

The vertical's value is bounded between 0 and the strike width, $20, so its price divided by the width approximates the market's (risk-neutral, discounted) odds of finishing above the strikes. The butterfly is bounded between 0 and its wing width, $10, and its price divided by that width approximates the risk-neutral probability of finishing near 780; divide once more by the width and you have the density. Toggle the valuation date: at 14 days the vertical and butterfly curves are still flat versions of their expiry shapes, because their short legs offset most of the long legs' time value.

</Pro>

## What a spread does to the Greeks

The payoff chart shows outcomes at expiry. Greeks show what the position is exposed to **today**. Spreads change the mix dramatically, and comparing per dollar of premium makes the shift obvious.

<G1GreekProfile client:visible />
<SnapshotNote detail="model Greeks, each strike at its own implied volatility" />

Per $1,000 of premium:

- The **call** buys 39 shares of delta, 0.90 of gamma, and **+$64 of vega**. It pays about $18 a day in decay.
- The **vertical** buys 28 shares of delta but almost no gamma (0.04) and just $15 of vega. It is a nearly pure directional position: the short 790 call cancels most of the volatility exposure.
- The **butterfly** is almost delta-neutral at today's price (−2.4 shares) and has **negative** gamma and **−$65 of vega**. It *earns* about $10 a day. Per dollar, it is as much a volatility position as the call, with the opposite sign.

Switch to "per contract" and the butterfly's Greeks look tiny. That is the trap: they are small because the position is small. Scaled to equal capital, the butterfly is a large short-volatility bet.

<Plain>

Use the dashboard words. The call's **speedometer** (delta) says it moves about 54 cents per dollar of SPY. Its **sensitivity to the forecast** (vega) is large: if the weather forecast for price swings gets stormier, the call gains.

The vertical keeps most of the speedometer but removes most of the forecast sensitivity. You are betting on direction, not on storms.

The butterfly flips the picture. Its speedometer barely moves right now, its **melting ice** (theta) works *for* you, and a stormier forecast *hurts* it. It wins if SPY stays calm and drifts to 780.

</Plain>

<Pro>

For a position with legs $$i$$ and quantities $$n_i$$, every Greek is additive: $$\Delta_{\text{pos}} = \sum_i n_i \Delta_i$$, and likewise for $$\Gamma$$, $$\Theta$$ and $$\mathcal{V}$$. With model values at SPY 769.66:

$$
\Gamma_{770/790} = 0.0125 - 0.0121 = 0.0004, \qquad \mathcal{V}_{770/790} = 0.890 - 0.745 \approx 0.144
$$

per share. The 790 call's gamma and vega are nearly as large as the 770's, so the vertical retains delta (0.542 − 0.273) while shedding almost all of the convexity. A desk uses verticals when it wants delta without vega; it uses butterflies when it wants to sell gamma and vega in a defined-risk package. Note the vertical's small *positive* gamma only holds near the lower strike: as SPY rises toward 790, the short call dominates and the vertical's gamma turns negative.

</Pro>

## Four reasons to spread

<Plain>

1. **Cost.** Selling an option against the one you buy lowers the cash you put up.
2. **Defined risk.** Every structure in the chart has a known worst case. A short option on its own does not.
3. **Isolating one bet.** A vertical is mostly about direction; a straddle is mostly about how much SPY moves; a calendar is mostly about time and the forecast. Pick the shape that matches your actual opinion.
4. **Turning an edge into a position you can hold.** If you think one option is mispriced, buying it alone also buys direction and decay you never wanted. Pairing it with a fairly priced option hedges those away.

</Plain>

<Pro>

Professionals frame it this way: theoretical edge (price vs value under your volatility forecast, lesson [B6](/vol/b6/)) is only useful if the risk you carry to collect it is acceptable. A naked option with $0.30 of edge might carry 54 deltas and $89 of vega per contract. Spreading converts that into a position whose dominant risk is the one you have an opinion about. The price is reduced edge: the short leg gives some back, and you pay the bid-ask spread on twice as many legs. On the snapshot the 770 and 790 calls are each about $0.04–0.06 wide, so the vertical's round-trip slippage is around $10 per spread against a $946 premium, small here but meaningful in less liquid names. Module G walks through each spread family; [G2](/vol/g2/) sorts them by what they do to gamma and vega.

</Pro>

## Common mistakes

- **Judging a spread by its per-contract Greeks.** A butterfly's −$11 of vega looks negligible until you trade enough of them to match the capital in one call.
- **Assuming a debit spread is "long volatility" because you paid for it.** The 770/780/790 butterfly is a debit with negative vega and gamma.
- **Ignoring where the Greeks flip.** A vertical's gamma and vega change sign as SPY moves between the strikes. The profile above is a snapshot at 769.66.
- **Forgetting the extra legs cost money.** Every leg pays half a bid-ask spread to enter and again to exit.

<Takeaway>

- A spread trades upside for lower cost and a defined worst case.
- Greeks add across legs; the short leg cancels part of the long leg's exposure.
- The 770/790 vertical keeps half the call's delta with a sixth of its vega: a near-pure directional bet.
- The 770/780/790 butterfly is cheap, nearly delta-neutral, and short gamma and vega: a bet on SPY settling near 780.
- Compare structures per dollar of premium, not per contract.

</Takeaway>
