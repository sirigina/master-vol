On October 27, 2026, Microsoft options expiring three days later were priced at **75%** implied volatility, while the same strike a month and a half out was priced at 30%. Both describe the same stock. The difference is one day: earnings. This lesson explains how implied volatility varies across expiries, how to split it into pieces, and why buying options into earnings so often disappoints.

## The term structure

<Plain>

Implied volatility is a weather forecast, and every expiry carries its own forecast for the period up to that date. Line them up by date and you get the **term structure**.

- **Upward sloping** (normal): near-term options are priced calmer than long-term ones. Today is quiet, but over a year anything can happen.
- **Inverted**: near-term options are priced *stormier* than long-term ones. That happens when a storm is already here (a selloff) or is scheduled (an earnings report, an election, a central-bank decision).

</Plain>

<Pro>

The term structure is the map $$T \mapsto \sigma_{\text{ATM}}(T)$$. In calm markets equity index term structures slope upward because short-dated implied vol sits near the currently low realized vol while long-dated vol is anchored near the long-run mean plus a term premium. In stressed markets they invert: front-month implied vol jumps with realized vol, while mean reversion ([lesson C6](/vol/c6/)) keeps long-dated vol from following. VIX futures curves (contango vs backwardation) show the same pattern in tradeable form.

</Pro>

SPY's term structure on the snapshot is a textbook upward slope beyond the first month: **14.06%** for November 27 (31 days), **14.74%** for December 18, **15.86%** for March 19, and **18.09%** for December 2027 (416 days). The front few expiries are flat around 14% to 15%.

## Forward volatility

<Plain>

If you know the forecast for the next month and the forecast for the next two months, you can work out the forecast for *the second month alone*. That number is **forward volatility**: the storm the market is pricing for a future window.

The key idea: what adds up over time is not volatility but **variance** (volatility squared, times time), just like the coin-flip walks in [lesson C1](/vol/c1/). So you add and subtract variance, then convert back.

</Plain>

<Pro>

Total implied variance to expiry $$T_i$$ is $$\sigma_i^2T_i$$. Variance over disjoint periods adds, so the variance between $$T_1$$ and $$T_2$$ is the difference, and

$$
\sigma_{1\to2} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_2^2T_2 - \sigma_1^2T_1}{T_2 - T_1}}
$$

From November 27 (31 days, 14.06%) to December 18 (52 days, 14.74%): $$\sqrt{(0.1474^2 \times 52 - 0.1406^2 \times 31)/21} = 15.69\%$$. The December options are pricing the three weeks after November 27 at almost 16%, higher than either quoted IV. A forward variance below zero would be an arbitrage (a calendar spread with negative cost), so total variance must increase with $$T$$. The calculation above uses calendar days, matching how the IVs were quoted; desks often weight days instead, giving weekends and holidays less variance than trading days.

</Pro>

Pick November 20 and November 27 in the calculator. The forward vol for that week is only **12.8%**, a dip. The gap contains Thanksgiving (November 26) and the half-day after it, and quoted IVs count every calendar day equally. Fewer trading sessions, less variance to price. This is the simplest example of uneven variance across days.

## Events concentrate variance

Now the main event. MSFT reports earnings on October 28 after the close. The stock will absorb a quarter's worth of news in one session.

<Plain>

Think of the October 30 options as a three-day forecast in which one of the days is a hurricane. Spread over three days, that hurricane makes the whole forecast look extreme: **75%** volatility. The November 6 options contain the same hurricane but spread over ten days, so they show 45%. December 18 spreads it over 52 days: 30%.

The straddle gives the quick read. The October 30 530 straddle costs **$27.07**, or **5.1%** of MSFT's price. Traders call that the **implied move**: the market expects MSFT to move about 5% either way on the report.

</Plain>

<Pro>

Model each expiry's total variance as $$n-1$$ ordinary days plus one event day:

$$
\sigma_i^2\,\frac{T_i}{365} = \sigma_n^2\,\frac{T_i - 1}{365} + m^2
$$

where $$\sigma_n$$ is the normal-day (ex-earnings) vol and $$m$$ the standard deviation of the event-day return. With the front expiry alone and an assumed $$\sigma_n = 25\%$$: $$m = \sqrt{0.748^2 \times 3/365 - 0.25^2 \times 2/365} = 6.52\%$$. A least-squares fit across all four expiries solves for both unknowns: $$\sigma_n = 25.3\%$$ and $$m = 6.42\%$$. The expected absolute move is $$\sqrt{2/\pi}\,m \approx 0.8m$$, about 5.1% to 5.2%, consistent with the straddle's 5.1%. One earnings day carries roughly **24 normal days** of variance ($$m^2 / (\sigma_n^2/365) \approx 23.6$$ at the fitted values).

</Pro>

Drag the normal-day volatility. Between 20% and 30% the earnings move from the front expiry barely changes: it stays between **±6.4% and ±6.6%**, because the event dominates a three-day option. The later expiries are far more sensitive to that assumption: near 25% all four expiries agree on a move of about **6.4% to 6.6%**, which is why the fit lands there. Push to 30% and the December expiry can only fit a 2.3% move; the four expiries stop telling a consistent story.

## The crush

Once earnings are out, the hurricane is gone. Implied volatility collapses overnight to something like the normal-day level. That is the **IV crush**, and it is why buying a straddle into earnings needs a big move just to break even.

<Plain>

Buy the October 30 straddle for $27.07 ($2,707 per contract). Suppose MSFT reports and **doesn't move**, and implied vol drops to 30%. At the close on October 29, the day after the report, the straddle is worth about $6.74; you've lost **$2,033**, three quarters of what you paid. If vol had stayed at 75% the loss would have been only about $1,046: the crush is responsible for roughly half the damage, and two days of time decay plus the passing of the event for the rest.

To make money, MSFT has to move more than the market expected. At 30% post-earnings IV the breakevens are about **−5.4% and +4.8%**. A 3% rise, which sounds big, still loses about **$961** (a 3% fall loses about $1,228). An 8% rise makes about **$1,683**.

Buying options before earnings is a bet that the move will be bigger than the implied move, not just a bet on "a big move".

</Plain>

<Pro>

The long straddle is long the event variance $$m^2$$ priced at 6.4%-6.5%, and long normal-day variance priced near 25%. After the announcement only the normal-day variance remains, so the straddle reprices from 75% to roughly $$\sigma_n$$ almost regardless of the outcome. The P/L decomposes as

$$
\text{P/L} \approx \underbrace{\tfrac12\Gamma\,(\Delta S)^2}_{\text{realized event move}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{Vega}\times\Delta\sigma}_{\text{crush},\ \Delta\sigma<0} \;+\; \underbrace{\Theta\,\Delta t}_{\text{decay},\ \Theta<0}
$$

and the trade has positive expectation only if the realized event move exceeds the implied $$m$$ by enough to cover the crush. Notice the breakevens are **asymmetric** (−5.4% vs +4.8%): the 530 strike sits below spot, so the call is in the money and gains on an up move start from a higher base. The post-earnings IV slider matters less than you might expect for large moves (deep ITM options carry little vega) and most at small moves. Desks trade this with **calendar spreads** (short the event expiry, long the next) that isolate the event variance from normal-day variance ([lesson G6](/vol/g6/)).

</Pro>

## Common mistakes

- **Averaging volatilities across expiries.** Variance adds, volatility doesn't. Use $$\sigma^2T$$ to combine or split periods.
- **Reading a 75% front-week IV as "MSFT is a 75% vol stock".** It is one big day spread across three. The normal-day vol is closer to 25%.
- **Buying straddles because "earnings will move it".** The market already prices the move. You need a move bigger than about 5% just to break even here.
- **Ignoring holidays and weekends in short-dated forward vol.** A gap with fewer sessions prices less variance.

<Takeaway>

- The term structure is implied vol by expiry. SPY's slopes upward: 14.06% at 31 days to 18.09% at 416 days.
- Forward volatility comes from variance additivity: subtract total variances and divide by the time between.
- A scheduled event concentrates variance on one day and inflates short-dated IV the most.
- MSFT's chain implies an earnings-day move of about ±6.4% (one SD), about 24 normal days of variance; the straddle prices about a 5.1% move.
- After the event IV crushes toward the normal-day level. A long straddle needs a move bigger than the implied move to profit.

</Takeaway>
