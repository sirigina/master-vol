A conversion uses stock. Take the stock out and combine two synthetics instead, and you get the box: four options that pay a fixed amount at expiry, no matter what. A box is a loan written in options, and its price tells you the interest rate.

## The box: two synthetics that cancel

<Plain>

Buy the synthetic stock at a low strike (long 750 call, short 750 put) and sell it at a high strike (short 790 call, long 790 put). At expiry:

- The first pair makes you **buy SPY at 750**.
- The second pair makes you **sell SPY at 790**.

The shares come and go; you keep **$40**, every time, at any price. A position that pays a guaranteed $40 in 31 days should cost a little less than $40 today, the way a savings bond does. How much less tells you the interest rate.

Another way to see the same four options: a **bull call spread** (long 750 call, short 790 call) plus a **bear put spread** (long 790 put, short 750 put).

</Plain>

<Pro>

For strikes $$K_1 < K_2$$, the long box pays $$K_2 - K_1$$ at expiry with no market risk, so for European options

$$
\text{Box} = (C_{K_1} - C_{K_2}) + (P_{K_2} - P_{K_1}) = (K_2 - K_1)\,e^{-rT}
$$

Solving for the rate gives the **box rate**,

$$
r_{\text{box}} = -\frac{1}{T}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{\text{Box price}}{K_2 - K_1}\right)
$$

No stock, no dividend, no borrow: a box isolates pure financing. Buying a box is lending (pay now, receive $$K_2-K_1$$ later); selling a box is borrowing. Exchange-listed European index boxes (SPX) are used this way as a funding market, and the box rate tracks Treasury bill rates plus a small spread.

</Pro>

<Plain>

Start with 750/790. A box that pays $40 in 31 days trades at a mid price of **$40.59**: more than it pays. That would be a negative interest rate, someone paying you to lend. Something else is going on.

The answer is that SPY options are **American**. The in-the-money 790 put can be exercised early, and that right is worth about **$0.70** on its own. The box buyer owns the 790 put and gets that right as a bonus, so the box costs more. Remove it and the box is worth **$39.89**, an implied rate of **3.35%**.

Now try 700/800, the widest box. It trades at **$100.92** at mid, again above its $100 payoff. Strip out $1.36 of early-exercise value and the rate is **5.15%**, close to the 5.0–5.1% American-model rate from [lesson F2](/vol/f2/).

</Plain>

<Pro>

Two lessons in the numbers:

1. **Rate sensitivity scales with width.** On a $40 box over 31 days, one cent of price is 0.30 points of rate; on a $100 box it's 0.12. A narrow box is a terrible rate instrument: the 770/780 box is $9.80 bid, $10.49 ask, which spans implied rates from +24% down to −56%. Desks trade wide boxes, and in size.
2. **American boxes are not riskless.** The early-exercise premium is real value, but it is also a risk: if you *sell* the box, the long put holder may exercise against you early, leaving you with stock instead of a clean loan ([lesson F5](/vol/f5/)). That is why boxes as funding trades use European, cash-settled index options.

The natural bid/ask (what you'd pay to buy, receive to sell) brackets a range of implied rates, often negative at the buy price and well above any real rate at the sell price. No-arbitrage only requires the reference rate to fall inside that range.

</Pro>

## The jelly roll: financing between two expiries

A box spans two strikes at one expiry. A **jelly roll** spans one strike across two expiries: long the synthetic in one month, short it in the other.

<Plain>

Buy the December 770 synthetic (long call, short put) and sell the November 770 synthetic. In November you are committed to *sell* SPY at 770; in December to *buy* it back at 770. Between those dates you're short the stock, holding $770 of cash.

So a jelly roll is a bet on what those **21 days** between November 27 and December 18 cost. Two things happen in that window: you earn interest on the $770, and if a dividend is paid, you owe it, because you're short the shares.

</Plain>

<JellyRoll client:visible />
<SnapshotNote detail="770 strike, Nov 27 and Dec 18, 2026 expiries; mid and bid/ask as quoted" />

<Pro>

For European options with no dividend, the roll is worth the difference in the present value of the strike between the two dates:

$$
\text{Roll} = (C - P)_{T_2} - (C - P)_{T_1} = K\left(e^{-rT_1} - e^{-rT_2}\right) - \text{PV}(D_{T_1 < t < T_2})
$$

At 4.6%, financing alone is worth **$2.03**. The market quotes the roll at **$0.95** at mid ($0.82 bid, $1.08 ask). Reading it as financing only gives a forward rate of **2.15%** between the expiries, which is nonsense: the gap is the December dividend.

Subtracting the whole $2.10 dividend (present value $2.09) overshoots, to −$0.06. Because the dividend goes ex on December 18, a December call holder can exercise early to capture it, which gives back part of its value. The American model with a $2.10 dividend values the roll at **$1.14**, just above the market's $1.08 ask. Slide the dividend: at about **$2.50** the model matches the $0.95 mid. Desks use exactly this calculation in reverse: hold the rate fixed and solve the roll for the market's **implied dividend**. Here it says the market is pricing a somewhat larger December dividend than our $2.10 guess, or slightly cheaper financing over those three weeks.

</Pro>

## Common mistakes

- **Treating an American equity box as riskless.** Its puts can be exercised early, breaking the box into stock and a spread.
- **Trading narrow boxes for the rate.** One cent on a $5 box over a month moves the rate by more than 2 points.
- **Reading a jelly roll as pure financing.** Any dividend between the expiries is in the price; ignore it and the implied forward rate is wrong by points.
- **Comparing box prices at mid only.** The natural price is what you'd get, and on SPY the spread is wider than the rate edge.

<Takeaway>

- A box (bull call spread + bear put spread) pays K2 − K1 at expiry with no market risk; its price is a loan's price.
- SPY's American puts add early-exercise value: the 700/800 box trades at $100.92 for a $100 payoff; removing $1.36 of early-exercise value gives a 5.15% rate.
- Wider boxes give more stable rates; narrow ones swing wildly with each cent.
- A jelly roll (long synthetic in one expiry, short in another) prices the financing and dividends between the two dates.
- November vs December 770: financing alone $2.03, market $0.95, an American model with a $2.10 December dividend $1.14; the market implies a dividend nearer $2.50.

</Takeaway>
