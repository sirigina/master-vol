If you learn only one Greek, learn delta. It tells you how much your option's price moves when the stock moves, and that one number gets used four different ways on a trading desk.

## Delta as speed

<Plain>

Delta is the option's **speedometer relative to the stock**. A delta of 0.54 means: when SPY moves $1, this option moves about 54 cents.

- A call that is deep in the money (very likely to pay off) moves almost dollar-for-dollar with the stock: delta near **1**.
- A call far out of the money (a long shot) barely reacts: delta near **0**.
- Puts move the *opposite* way to the stock, so their deltas are **negative**, between 0 and −1.

</Plain>

<Pro>

Delta is the first derivative of option value with respect to the underlying price:

$$
\Delta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}, \qquad \Delta_{\text{call}} = e^{-qT} N(d_1), \qquad \Delta_{\text{put}} = \Delta_{\text{call}} - e^{-qT}
$$

With no dividend before expiry, call delta minus put delta at the same strike is 1. Check it on the snapshot: the 770 call shows **+0.54** and the 770 put **−0.47**. The gap is 1.01; the extra 0.01 is rounding and the quotes not being perfectly simultaneous.

</Pro>

## How delta changes with price, time, and volatility

Delta isn't fixed. Move SPY and it changes; let time pass and it changes; change volatility and it changes. Use the controls to see all three.

<DeltaCurves client:visible />
<SnapshotNote detail="model curves for the 770 strike, rate implied from the chain" />

Three patterns to notice:

1. **The S-curve.** Delta rises from 0 to 1 as SPY passes the strike. That changing slope is **gamma**, the next lesson.
2. **Time sharpens it.** With 31 days left, the curve is gentle. With 2 days left, it is almost a step: the option is about to become either worth something or nothing.
3. **Volatility softens it.** Higher volatility means more uncertainty about where SPY ends up, so even far-away options keep some delta. Slide volatility to 40% and watch the 31-day curve flatten.

<Plain>

The speedometer reading is not fixed. Near the strike it changes quickly as SPY moves; close to expiry it jumps toward full speed (1) or a standstill (0); and when the forecast turns stormier, every option's reading drifts toward the middle, because nothing looks settled.

</Plain>

<Pro>

Each pattern is a second-order Greek. The change with price is gamma, $$\partial\Delta/\partial S$$ ([lesson D2](/vol/d2/)); the change with time is charm, $$\partial\Delta/\partial t$$; the change with volatility is vanna, $$\partial\Delta/\partial\sigma$$, positive for out-of-the-money calls and negative for in-the-money ones. [Lesson D6](/vol/d6/) maps all three. A delta hedge is only as good as the last time these effects were netted out.

</Pro>

## Delta as a hedge ratio and a share equivalent

This is how professionals actually use delta. A position's total delta tells you **how many shares of stock it behaves like**, and therefore how many shares to trade to neutralize the price risk.

<DeltaHedge client:visible />
<SnapshotNote detail="Nov 27, 2026 expiry, snapshot IV held fixed" />

Ten 770 calls at delta 0.54 behave like owning about **540 shares** of SPY. Sell 540 shares and the position is *delta-neutral*: a small move in SPY barely changes its value.

Now move SPY by $10 or $20. The delta estimate and the full reprice drift apart, and the hedged P/L is **positive in both directions**. That gap is gamma at work. A long option position gets longer as the market rises and shorter as it falls, which is exactly why traders pay time decay to own it (lessons [D2](/vol/d2/) and [D7](/vol/d7/)).

<Plain>

The share-equivalent idea makes options comparable to stock. Owning 10 of these calls is, *right now*, like owning 540 shares, but for about $13,900 instead of $415,000. The catch is in "right now": the share count changes as the price moves and as time passes.

</Plain>

<Pro>

Delta is a local, linear approximation. For a move $$\Delta S$$ the change in option value is better approximated by

$$
\Delta V \approx \Delta \cdot \Delta S + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Gamma\,(\Delta S)^2
$$

so a delta-neutral long-option position earns $$\tfrac{1}{2}\Gamma(\Delta S)^2$$ on any move, at the cost of theta while it waits. A hedge set at one price is wrong at the next; maintaining it ("dynamic hedging") is the core activity of an options market maker and the subject of module E.

</Pro>

## Delta as a rough probability

Traders often say a 0.16-delta option has "about a 16% chance" of finishing in the money. The 800 call on the snapshot has delta **0.16**. It is a useful rule of thumb, with two caveats.

<Pro>

- The precise risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money is $$N(d_2)$$, not $$N(d_1)$$. For short-dated options they are close; for long-dated or high-volatility options they diverge.
- It is a risk-neutral probability (the model grows SPY at the financing rate, not at its real expected return), not a real-world forecast. Your broker's "chance of profit" is different again because it subtracts the premium.

</Pro>

<Plain>

Rule of thumb: **delta ≈ odds of finishing in the money**. At-the-money options are roughly 50/50 (delta near 0.5). A 0.10-delta option is a long shot, about 1 in 10. It's good enough for quick intuition; don't use it for precise risk.

</Plain>

## Common mistakes

- **Treating delta as constant.** It is only accurate for small moves and short periods.
- **Forgetting the multiplier.** Delta 0.54 on one contract is 54 shares of exposure, not 0.54 shares.
- **Ignoring sign.** Long puts and short calls both carry negative delta: both make money when SPY falls.
- **Reading delta as a real-world probability.** It is a model quantity under risk-neutral assumptions.

<Takeaway>

- Delta = how much the option price moves per $1 move in the underlying.
- Calls: 0 to 1. Puts: 0 to −1. Call delta − put delta = 1 at the same strike (no dividends).
- Position delta × 100 = share-equivalent exposure; trade the opposite to hedge.
- Delta changes with price (gamma), time, and volatility. Short-dated options have step-like deltas.
- Delta ≈ rough odds of finishing in the money, but the exact figure is N(d₂).

</Takeaway>
