You own 100 shares of SPY at 769.66, about $77,000 of stock. You want to keep the upside but sleep through a selloff. Options give you three basic tools for this, and each one trades protection, cost and upside in a different way.

## The protective put: buying insurance

<Plain>

A put is an **insurance policy** on your shares. You pay a premium now; if SPY falls below the strike by expiration, the policy pays the difference.

With SPY at 769.66, the November 27 750 put costs **$5.94 a share**, or $594 for 100 shares. That buys you a floor: however far SPY falls, you can sell at 750. Your worst case is the $19.66 drop to 750 plus the $5.94 premium, a loss of **$2,560**. Without the put, a crash to 650 would cost you $11,966.

The price is the deductible plus the premium. A put closer to today's price has a smaller deductible and a bigger premium. A put further away is cheap, but you absorb more of the fall yourself.

</Plain>

<Pro>

Stock plus a long put has the same expiration payoff as a long call at the same strike plus cash (put-call parity, [lesson F2](/vol/f2/)):

$$
S + P(K) = C(K) + K e^{-rT}
$$

So the protected position is long a call in disguise: delta below 1, positive gamma, negative theta, long vega. At expiry,

$$
\text{P/L} = \max(S_T, K) - S_0 - P, \qquad \text{worst case} = K - S_0 - P, \qquad \text{breakeven} = S_0 + P
$$

For the 750 November put: worst case $$750 - 769.66 - 5.94 = -25.60$$ a share, breakeven 775.60. The insurance has to be earned back by a rally before the position shows a profit.

</Pro>

## The covered call: selling upside for income

<Plain>

A covered call is the opposite trade. You **collect** a premium by selling someone the right to buy your shares at a higher price. With the November 27 790 call at $4.44, you take in $444.

That money is a cushion, not a floor. It absorbs the first $4.44 of a fall; below 765.22 you lose dollar for dollar like a plain shareholder. And if SPY rallies past 790, your gains stop there.

A covered call is a partial hedge at best. It suits a view that the market will drift sideways, not a fear of a crash.

</Plain>

<Pro>

Stock plus a short call is synthetically a short put ([lesson F1](/vol/f1/)): $$S - C(K) = -P(K) + K e^{-rT}$$. The position is short gamma and short vega, the exact reverse of the protective put. Delta falls from 1 to about $$1 - 0.27 = 0.73$$ with the 790 call (delta 0.271), so it hedges about a quarter of the price risk for small moves and less in a crash, because the short call's delta shrinks toward zero as SPY falls.

The trade-off is explicit in the payoff: maximum gain $$K - S_0 + C = 790 - 769.66 + 4.44 = 24.78$$ a share, and a loss that runs all the way down to zero, less the premium.

</Pro>

## The collar: let the call pay for the put

<Plain>

A collar combines the two: buy a put below the market, sell a call above it, and use the call money to pay for the insurance.

On the snapshot, the November 27 **740 put and 790 call both cost $4.44**. Buy one, sell the other, and the protection is free in cash terms. You give up everything above 790 to get a floor at 740. Your result at expiry is boxed in between a loss of $2,966 and a gain of $2,034, whatever happens.

"Zero cost" is a description of the cash flow, not a free lunch. You paid with the upside you sold.

</Plain>

<Pro>

The collar's expiry payoff equals a long bull call spread $$C(K_1) - C(K_2)$$ plus cash $$K_1 e^{-rT}$$. Its Greeks are the stock's delta of 1 minus the put and call deltas: $$1 - 0.196 - 0.271 = 0.53$$ for the 740/790, so roughly half the market exposure for a small move.

Skew matters here. The 740 put trades at 17.9% implied volatility and the 790 call at 12.2%. At a single flat 14.1% volatility the 790 call ($5.91) would be worth far more than the 740 put ($2.31), and the call that pays for the 740 put would sit near 807, about 4.9% above SPY instead of 2.6%. Because index puts are rich ([lesson C8](/vol/c8/)), a collar buyer sells relatively cheap upside to buy relatively expensive downside. That is why many zero-cost index collars cap the upside closer to spot than the floor sits below it: here the floor is 3.9% below SPY and the cap only 2.6% above.

</Pro>

Switch the builder to **Collar** and pick a put strike; the note under the chart tells you which call comes closest to paying for it. In December, the 750 put ($9.68) is almost covered by the 790 call ($8.54), a net debit of $1.14.

## The time cost of insurance

<Plain>

Insurance is priced per period, and short policies are expensive per day. The question to ask is **what it costs per year** if you keep renewing it.

The chart below shows two views. By strike: the further below the market the strike sits, the cheaper the put, but the decline is steep at first and then flattens, because far-away puts carry high implied volatility (the 650 put is priced at 32.1% volatility against 14.0% for the 770 put). By expiry: the at-the-money put for 10 days costs 0.90% of SPY, which works out to **32.7% a year** if you rolled it forever. The 416-day put costs 5.90%, or **5.2% a year**.

</Plain>

<Pro>

At constant volatility, at-the-money option value grows with $$\sqrt{T}$$ (about $$0.4\,S\sigma\sqrt{T}$$), so the annualized cost scales as $$1/\sqrt{T}$$. Over the listed expiries the 770 put's price rises 6.6× for 41.6× the time, slightly more than the 6.4× that $$\sqrt{T}$$ predicts, because the term structure slopes upward (14.5% to 18.6% put IV).

Two cautions when comparing annualized figures:

- **Rolling short-dated at-the-money puts is not the same product** as one long-dated put. Each roll resets the strike to the new spot, so the cover follows the market up and protects gains; a one-year put struck at 770 does not. Part of the extra cost buys that reset.
- **Theta is front-loaded.** An at-the-money option loses value fastest in its last weeks ([lesson D3](/vol/d3/)). Hedgers who need continuous cover often buy two to six months out and roll before the last month, trading a higher upfront premium for slower decay.

</Pro>

## Common mistakes

- **Calling a collar "free".** The premium nets to zero; the cost is the upside you sold, and on index collars skew means you usually sell more upside than you buy downside.
- **Treating a covered call as a hedge.** It cushions a small dip by the premium received, nothing more. Below the cushion, losses run all the way down to zero.
- **Comparing put prices across expiries without annualizing.** A cheap-looking one-week put can be the most expensive insurance per day you can buy.
- **Forgetting the breakeven moves.** A protective put pushes your breakeven above today's price by the premium; you need a rally just to stand still.

<Takeaway>

- Protective put = floor at the strike minus the premium; it turns stock into a synthetic long call.
- Covered call = premium cushion and a cap on gains; it is a synthetic short put, not crash insurance.
- Collar = long put plus short call; on the snapshot the Nov 27 740 put and 790 call both price at $4.44, a zero-cost collar with a $2,966 floor and a $2,034 cap.
- Put skew makes downside strikes expensive, which pulls zero-cost collar caps closer to spot.
- Insurance cost scales roughly with √time, so short-dated puts are far more expensive per year (32.7% a year for 10 days at the money vs 5.2% for 416 days).

</Takeaway>
